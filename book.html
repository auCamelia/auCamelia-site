Modelling

Six cases, proven in simulation.

Examples of Modelling for Decision Making in practice: an AI-driven robotic control architecture, a machine-vision proof of concept, a quadruped digital twin, a 12-drone swarm and an eddy-current hole-location bench and a manual-versus-hybrid trade-off study — with more to come.

01 — Natural language

Natural-language robot control, ROS 2 & PDDL task planning.

A simulation of a test bench built on the ROS 2 framework, using a PDDL solver for task planning. A user states a goal in plain language — "measure h8" — and the system plans and executes the sequence itself, instead of a hand-written program.

The test bench is built around a Fanuc CRX-20 cobot fitted with a Stäubli quick-change interface, letting the robot swap its own end-effectors depending on the task.

It's also the proof point for a broader capability: complex machines — measurement systems, robotic cells — are often only operable by a small group of specialists, a bottleneck of dependency, long training, and equipment left under-used outside the hours those specialists are available. The test bench's natural-language layer shows that a plain instruction can be translated into an executable plan without sacrificing the rigour of the underlying control — a capability that extends wherever a complex machine could be opened up to more of the team.

02 — Machine vision

Sub-millimetre relocation, validated in simulation before hardware.

A splice-relocation concept based on a Zivid 2 camera, validated using Gazebo simulation before committing to physical testing. Targets: lateral position < 0.5 mm, tilt < 1°, distance to surface < 0.1 mm, with Cp > 1.66 and Cpk > 1.33.

The modelling validates the concept before investing in a representative proof of concept for qualification — the same discipline the method applies everywhere else.

03 — Digital twin

A quadruped digital twin to validate development before deployment.

DOGZILLA is a 12-DOF quadruped (3 joints × 4 legs) fitted with an IMU and a LiDAR.

It walks at a trot: diagonal pairs of legs (front-left / hind-right, front-right / hind-left) swing together, offset by half a cycle. Each leg stays on the ground 60 % of the cycle, so two legs are down 80 % of the time and four for the remaining 20 %. The cycle frequency ranges from 2 to 5 Hz depending on the desired speed.

Actions are uploaded to the STM32 controller and accessed through the DOGZILLALib library. The motors' position is only refreshed every ~130 ms, and the inverse kinematics behind the trot is not directly accessible. Hence the architecture of the twin: it exposes the same API as the real robot and rebuilds the gait and the kinematics itself from the commands the robot receives, not from its sensors.

04 — Drone swarm

Twelve Crazyflie drones, simulated with the genuine firmware controller.

System architecture: A swarm of 12 Bitcraze Crazyflie 2.1+ nano-drones built as a digital twin, to write and test formation scenarios before entrusting them to real drones. The simulation does not re-implement the flight controller: it compiles the C controller supplied with the Crazyflie firmware for the PC and loops it on a rigid-body dynamic, so trajectories behave as they would on the drone.

Simulation optimization: With two drones it first ran at 0.27× real time: the cost came from Python and the ROS executive, not from the physics. A scalar dynamics step (identical to the original within 3·10−13 over 20,000 steps), no unneeded conversions, a control loop at 500 Hz as in the firmware and several physics steps per timer call bring it to real time up to about 10 drones, and 0.91× at 12.

Formation set-up: Before each formation (circle, grid, cube, sphere, pyramid…), every drone must be given a slot. The Hungarian algorithm assigns them by minimising the sum of squared distances: with straight-line trips flown at the same time, paths do not cross.

05 — Eddy currents

Locating hidden holes under an aluminium plate with eddy currents.

Principle: An eddy-current probe is a coil driven by an alternating current. Near a conductive metal, it induces currents whose opposing field changes the impedance of the coil, and a hole, a crack or corrosion disturbs the signal. These currents decay with depth (skin effect): in 2024-T3 aluminium, 8 kHz gives a 1.35 mm skin depth and lets the probe see through 2 mm (field attenuation ×0.23), where 100 kHz (0.38 mm, ×0.005) would hide the flaw — low frequency to look deep, high frequency to look at the surface.

Bench and exercise: Two stacked 2 mm aluminium plates: the bottom one carries 31 pilot holes of Ø3.2 mm, the solid top one hides them (drawn transparent in the video). A Zaber XYZ gantry carries a head with three Lion Precision U5B probes at 120°, on a 6 mm radius circle, 1 mm above the surface. Hole positions are drawn at random for each campaign within ±5 mm of nominal, like a realistic assembly tolerance, and the probe never knows them.

Locating a hole: The signal is not strongest over the centre of the hole: the currents are concentric loops with none on the axis of the coil, so the response peaks when the hole is about 2 mm from the probe axis and dips slightly when it is centred. At each head position the software adds the three averaged readings to those already collected, fits the hole centre against a calibrated response curve and moves the head onto it, until two successive fits agree within 0.05 mm — three positions on average. The curve is learned beforehand by a calibration (7 × 7 grid, 147 readings over a dedicated hole, about 2.5 minutes), so changing the probe, frequency, material or lift-off means rerunning it, not changing the code.

Simulation: The signal is computed in Gazebo by a plugin from a table built offline with xfemm, the open-source finite-element solver derived from FEMM. A harmonic axisymmetric model (8 kHz) of the coil above the two plates includes the reaction of the plate on the field. An off-axis hole is not axisymmetric, so it is assembled from rings: the change of coil impedance is read for a thin ring removed at each radius, then summed with the area of the hole inside each annulus. The sum matches a direct run of the centred hole to 0.4 %.

Results: On a 31-hole campaign with a 0.1 mm target, the mean error is 0.024 mm (σ = 0.013 mm, maximum 0.062 mm), no hole falls outside tolerance, with Cp = 2.55 and Cpk = 1.94 against the usual thresholds of 1.33 and 1.67. Locating each hole takes about 16 s, 8 minutes for the 31.

06 — Trade-off

Comparing manual, hybrid and automated options on several criteria at once.

Principle: The same planning core drives the decision-comparison work of Modelling for Decision Making: different allocations of tasks between operator and machine are modelled, and the resulting plans are compared on cycle time, cost, ergonomics and flexibility — side by side, not one estimate at a time.

Case: Fastening two aircraft fuselage sections along their orbital joint. A fully manual process is compared with a hybrid one assisted by a gantry, four operators each, using a Gantt chart that shows the makespan and the occupancy of every resource.
